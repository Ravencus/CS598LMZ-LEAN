1. Prove the easy direction: if \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\), then for any \(\varepsilon>0\) one may take \(\alpha_\varepsilon=\beta_\varepsilon=f\), and the integral of \(\alpha_\varepsilon-\beta_\varepsilon\) is \(0\).

2. For the converse, start from integrable bounds \(\alpha,\beta\) with \(\alpha\le f\le \beta\) on \([a,b]\), and show that \(\beta-\alpha\) is Riemann integrable and nonnegative on \([a,b]\).

3. Show that, because \(\beta-\alpha\ge 0\) on \([a,b]\), the quantity \(\left|\int_a^b (\alpha-\beta)\right|\) is exactly the “gap integral” \(\int_a^b (\beta-\alpha)\), so the hypothesis gives arbitrarily small total gap between the lower and upper bounds.

4. Prove the key criterion: if for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exist Riemann integrable functions \(\alpha,\beta\) with \(\alpha\le f\le \beta\) on \([a,b]\) and \(\int_a^b(\beta-\alpha)<\varepsilon\), then \(f\) is Riemann integrable (equivalently, its upper and lower Riemann integrals coincide).

5. Conclude the reverse implication by applying the criterion from the previous step to the bounds supplied by the hypothesis.
