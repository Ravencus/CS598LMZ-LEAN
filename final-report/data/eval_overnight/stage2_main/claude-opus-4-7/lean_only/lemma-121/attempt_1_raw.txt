Looking at this theorem carefully, I need to consider whether it's actually true.

The theorem claims that polynomials in $z$ are uniformly dense in $C(\mathbb{T}, \mathbb{C})$. However, this is **false** as a mathematical statement. Consider $f(z) = \bar{z}$ on the unit circle. For any polynomial $P(z) = \sum_k a_k z^k$:

The Fourier coefficient at index $-1$ of $P(e^{i\theta}) - e^{-i\theta}$ is $0 - 1 = -1$, since $P$ has no negative-index terms. But if $\|P - \bar{z}\|_\infty < \varepsilon$, then $|\widehat{(P-\bar{z})}(-1)| \leq \varepsilon$, forcing $\varepsilon \geq 1$.

The classical Stone-Weierstrass theorem on $\mathbb{T}$ requires the algebra to be **self-adjoint** (closed under conjugation), which polynomials in $z$ alone are not — Laurent polynomials (or $\{z, \bar{z}\}$) are needed. The disk algebra (closure of polynomials on $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$) is a proper subspace of $C(\mathbb{T})$.
